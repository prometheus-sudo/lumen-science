# Energy flow in ecosystems

**Field:** ecology · **Module:** Ecosystem Science · **Concept id:** eco-energy

---

Energy is the currency that makes ecological structure possible. Without a continuous input of usable energy, the ordered patterns of forests, reefs and grasslands would dissolve into thermodynamic equilibrium. This chapter explains how energy enters ecosystems, how it is transformed and lost, how ecologists measure the flows, and why the shape of energy budgets predicts so much about food webs, biomass and human dependence on primary production.

## Entry points: light and chemistry

Most ecosystems are powered by sunlight. Photosynthetic organisms intercept photons and store a fraction of that energy in the chemical bonds of organic molecules. A minority of ecosystems, such as those at hydrothermal vents, are powered by chemical energy. Gross primary productivity is total fixation; net primary productivity is what remains after producer respiration—the energy available to herbivores and detrital pathways.

## One-way flow

Usable energy is not recycled like nutrients. It enters, is transformed through feeding and decomposition, and is lost as heat. Materials cycle; energy flows. That distinction prevents the most common confusion in ecology teaching.

## Secondary production and efficiency

Consumers convert food into biomass inefficiently. Teaching often uses about ten percent transfer between trophic levels as a rule of thumb. Real efficiencies vary, but losses accumulate, energy pyramids stay upright, and food chains stay short.

## Measurement and synthesis

Productivity is estimated with harvest methods, gas exchange, remote sensing and aquatic techniques. Units and time scales must be stated when systems are compared. Energy flow is the skeleton for food webs, conservation of higher consumers, and human appropriation of primary production.
